Reconstruction Fidelity — How Many Harmonics Are Needed?
What is reconstruction fidelity?
We take your audio and rebuild it from scratch using only math — starting with just 1 harmonic (sine wave), then 2, then 3... all the way to 32. At each step we measure how close the rebuilt sound is to the original using a quality score called SNR (Signal-to-Noise Ratio). The minimum number of harmonics needed to reach 30dB quality is called N*.

How Does It Work?

A simple explanation of the science behind the system.

The Big Idea

Every instrument has a unique "sound fingerprint" — just like a human fingerprint, but made of sound waves. When a sitar plays a note, it doesn't produce just one sound. It produces the main note plus many quieter tones above it called harmonics. The pattern of these harmonics — how loud each one is — is what makes a sitar sound different from a veena or a guitar.

We use a mathematical formula called CTFS (Continuous Time Fourier Series) to measure exactly how strong each harmonic is. We extract 32 harmonic strengths per frame of audio — these 32 numbers are the instrument's fingerprint.

Our 7-Step Pipeline
1
Get Audio Download Sitar (Raga Yaman & Bhairavi), Veena (Raga Bhairavi & Desh), and Guitar recordings. Convert from MP3 to WAV format.
2
Label Ragas (Our Novelty #1) Instead of analyzing random audio, we manually mark the "pakad" — the most characteristic phrase of each raga. We extract features only from these meaningful musical moments.
3
Extract Harmonics Split audio into 100ms frames. For each frame, detect the fundamental frequency (f₀) and compute 32 CTFS harmonic coefficients using DFT math.
4
Reconstruction Fidelity (Our Novelty #2) Rebuild the sound from N=1,2,3...32 harmonics. Measure quality (SNR) at each step. Find N* — the minimum harmonics needed for 30dB quality. This is physics-justified feature selection, not arbitrary.
5
Build Feature Vectors For each frame, compute how much it deviates from its instrument's average harmonic profile. This deviation vector is the feature fed into the classifier.
6
Train Classifiers Train SVM (Support Vector Machine) and kNN (k-Nearest Neighbors) on 4,030 frames using 5-fold cross validation. kNN achieves 96.45% accuracy.
7
ANOVA Raga Analysis (Our Novelty #3) Test whether the same instrument sounds harmonically different in different ragas. Results: 21-28 out of 32 harmonics change significantly — proving raga context matters.
What is CTFS?
CTFS stands for Continuous Time Fourier Series. It's a mathematical way to break any repeating sound into a sum of simple sine waves (harmonics).
x(t) = Σ cₙ · ejnω₀t

In plain English: any sound wave = (harmonic 1 strength × sine wave 1) + (harmonic 2 strength × sine wave 2) + ... and so on. The strengths |c₁|, |c₂|, ..., |c₃₂| are what make each instrument unique.

Sitar harmonics

Harmonics decay slowly because of sympathetic strings. Many harmonics remain strong → N* = 9

Veena harmonics

Clean, sparse spectrum. Harmonics drop sharply → N* = 9

Guitar harmonics

Steel strings create richer harmonic content. More harmonics stay significant → N* = 13

Why this works

These patterns are consistent across notes and ragas — the harmonic ratio is a physical property of the instrument, not the note.

What is Reconstruction Fidelity?
The core question: If you only keep the first N harmonics, how accurate is the rebuilt sound compared to the original?
1
Take original audio frame (100ms) A real recording of the instrument. This is our reference.
2
Rebuild using only N harmonics x̂_N(t) = c₁·cos(ω₀t) + c₂·cos(2ω₀t) + ... + cₙ·cos(Nω₀t) — pure math, no original audio used.
3
Measure quality using SNR formula SNR(N) = 10 · log₁₀( ||original||² / ||original − rebuilt||² ). Higher SNR = better match.
4
Find N* where SNR ≥ 30dB Keep increasing N until quality is good enough. That minimum N is N* — the instrument's harmonic complexity score.
Why does this matter? N* is not arbitrary — it's determined by the physics of the instrument. Using N* as the feature dimension means our classifier uses exactly the right amount of information — not too little, not too much.
